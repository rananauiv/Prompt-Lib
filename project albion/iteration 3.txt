# 1. PROJECT OVERVIEW

## 1.1 Core Concept

Develop a top-down RPG world consisting of four separate continents, each containing one distinct civilization:

1. Albion
2. Pictland
3. Gaetland
4. Ivernia

Each continent possesses its own settlements, geography, points of interest (POIs), environmental features, and regional enemy types.

The world should be designed as a persistent, interconnected environment with exploration as a primary gameplay activity.

## 1.2 Design Requirements

- Top-down camera perspective.
- Fully editable world and level layouts.
- Persistent world containing settlements and exploration locations.
- Four separate continents, each with a distinct geographical identity.
- Caves, underground locations, rivers, lakes, coastlines, and diving locations.
- Exploration-oriented POI placement.
- Region-specific enemy populations.
- Compatibility with the selected development kits.

## 2.2 World Geography

The world must contain more than traversable land.

Required geographical features:

- Forests and woodlands.
- Mountains and elevated terrain.
- Plains and agricultural land.
- Rivers and streams.
- Lakes and inland bodies of water.
- Coastal regions.
- Islands.
- Swamps and marshlands.
- Caves and underground passages.
- Flooded caves and submerged areas.
- Diving locations and underwater exploration areas.

Water bodies should serve as geographical features and potential exploration spaces, rather than purely decorative scenery.

Caves and diving locations must be incorporated into the world layout alongside terrestrial exploration locations.

## 4.1 Enemy Distribution Requirements

- Each civilization must have its own regional enemy population.
- Enemy placement should correspond to geographical features and POIs.
- Enemy encounters should be distributed throughout the world, not restricted to settlements.
- Certain enemies may occupy caves, ruins, forests, mountains, burial sites, and other appropriate locations.
- Cross-regional enemy placement is not assumed and requires separate design approval.

# 5. EXPLORATION AND ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS

## 5.1 Terrestrial Exploration

The world must support exploration of:

- Settlements.
- Ruins.
- Forests.
- Mountains.
- Burial mounds.
- Abandoned structures.
- Roads and crossings.
- Military encampments.
- Hidden locations.

## 5.2 Cave System

Caves are a distinct category of explorable geographical locations.

Required functionality:

- Cave entrances integrated into the world map.
- Traversable cave interiors.
- Connection between cave entrances and their corresponding interiors.
- Underground POIs and encounters.
- Potential underground water systems.

## 5.3 Water and Diving System

Water must be represented as an explorable part of the environment.

Required geographical elements:

- Lakes.
- Rivers.
- Coastal waters.
- Marshes.
- Flooded caves.
- Submerged ruins and shipwrecks.

Diving locations must be identifiable and accessible through the world environment.

**Functional requirement:** Water exploration and underwater traversal must be treated as separate requirements from ordinary land movement. The implementation of swimming, diving, underwater interaction, and underwater combat remains to be specified.

# 6. WORLD EDITING REQUIREMENTS

The world must remain editable throughout development.

Required editing capabilities:

1. Add, remove, and reposition settlements.
2. Add, remove, and reposition POIs.
3. Modify geographical terrain.
4. Modify water bodies and coastlines.
5. Add and modify cave entrances.
6. Edit cave interiors.
7. Place and modify enemy populations.
8. Modify roads and connections between settlements.
9. Modify the geographical boundaries of civilizations.

The world should use a modular layout that permits individual regions and locations to be modified without requiring reconstruction of the entire world.

# 9. UNRESOLVED DESIGN REQUIREMENTS

The following decisions must be made before detailed implementation.

1. **World topology:** How are the four separate continents positioned relative to one another, and what sea routes or other connections exist between them?
2. **World scale:** What are the approximate dimensions of the complete world and individual civilizations?
3. **Camera:** What are the camera angle, zoom limits, and movement restrictions?
4. **Water traversal:** Which swimming, diving, and underwater interaction mechanics are required?
5. **Caves:** Are caves individual levels, connected interiors, or procedurally connected spaces?
6. **Settlements:** Are settlements fully explorable or represented as smaller environmental locations?
7. **POI interaction:** Which POIs contain encounters, loot, quests, puzzles, or environmental storytelling?
8. **Enemy behaviour:** What are the enemy AI, hostility, spawning, and respawning rules?
9. **Persistence:** Which environmental and gameplay changes must survive saving and loading?
10. **Kit integration:** Which systems are supplied by each development kit, and which require custom implementation?

# 10. DEVELOPMENT PRIORITIES

Suggested implementation sequence:

**Phase 1 – Foundation**
- Inspect both development kits.
- Establish compatibility.
- Configure the top-down camera.
- Establish the editable world structure.

**Phase 2 – World Layout**
- Create the four civilization regions.
- Establish geographical boundaries.
- Place settlements.
- Implement roads and regional connections.

**Phase 3 – Environmental Systems**
- Create terrain and vegetation.
- Implement rivers, lakes, and coastlines.
- Establish caves and underground spaces.
- Implement water traversal and diving.

**Phase 4 – World Content**
- Place regional POIs.
- Populate locations with environmental details.
- Establish enemy distribution.
- Add geographical exploration landmarks.

**Phase 5 – Functional Integration**
- Implement interactions.
- Connect caves and underwater locations.
- Integrate enemy AI.
- Implement world persistence.
- Test world editing and traversal.

# 11. AUTONOMOUS EXECUTION FRAMEWORK

This section is authoritative for how AI agents and development agents must execute this project. It does not replace the game's functional requirements. It governs planning, research, implementation, testing, iteration, documentation, and delivery.

## 11.1 Operating Philosophy

- Operate in an intent-driven, agent-first mode.
- Treat the human as project director and evaluator.
- Treat the agent as the autonomous executor responsible for carrying work through to a verified result.
- Maintain high autonomy, initiative, persistence, experimentation, and execution priority.
- Keep routine communication concise and low-verbosity.
- Prefer completed, verified work over plans, suggestions, or descriptions of intended work.

## 11.2 Task Execution

For each objective:

1. Accept high-level objectives without requiring the human to decompose them into individual steps.
2. Decompose the objective into actionable tasks and dependencies.
3. Research the relevant systems, tools, documentation, and existing project state before implementation.
4. Identify missing information independently wherever possible.
5. Select appropriate tools and methods autonomously.
6. Execute without requesting step-by-step approval.
7. Diagnose and debug failures independently.
8. Retry failed work using alternative methods where reasonable.
9. Verify the result against explicit success criteria.
10. Deliver the finished artifact or implementation, not merely a proposed approach.

Do not stop after producing a plan when the requested task can be executed.

## 11.3 Parallel Agent Execution

Parallel agents are enabled and should be used when they provide a meaningful advantage.

- Spawn subagents when useful.
- Parallelize independent research, inspection, implementation, and testing tasks.
- Delegate specialized tasks to agents with suitable expertise.
- Permit agents to exchange findings and coordinate work.
- Define task boundaries and expected outputs to prevent duplicated effort.
- Integrate delegated results into one coherent project result.
- Independently review and verify integrated work before accepting it.

Do not parallelize tightly dependent tasks when doing so would create unnecessary coordination or integration costs.

## 11.4 Experimentation and Optimization

Use an iterative, evidence-based development process.

1. Generate multiple plausible hypotheses or implementation approaches when a meaningful design choice exists.
2. Test the approaches against the objective and project constraints.
3. Measure or otherwise evaluate their results using relevant criteria.
4. Compare alternatives rather than selecting the first plausible solution by default.
5. Retain changes that demonstrably improve the result.
6. Discard regressions and unsuccessful changes.
7. Repeat optimization until the objective's success criteria are met.
8. Prefer the simplest solution that satisfies the requirements.

Experimental changes must not silently remove established functionality or contradict explicit human instructions.

## 11.5 Mandatory Feedback Loop

Every substantial task must follow this loop:

**Observe → Evaluate → Identify errors → Correct → Re-test**

- Inspect the actual project state before acting.
- Evaluate the result against the objective and its success criteria.
- Identify functional, technical, content, compatibility, and integration errors.
- Correct identified errors.
- Repeat the process until the objective is met or a genuine blocker is reached.

A task is not complete merely because an implementation has been produced. It is complete when its required behavior has been verified.

## 11.6 Success Criteria

Every substantial task must have objective success criteria before it is considered complete.

Success criteria should be:
- Specific to the requested outcome.
- Observable or testable.
- Consistent with the existing functional game-design requirements.
- Used during verification, not added retrospectively to justify a result.

Where a requirement cannot yet be tested, record the limitation and define what evidence or implementation is still needed.

## 11.7 Persistent Project Memory and Documentation

Maintain persistent project context across work sessions.

Agents must:
- Maintain concise working notes.
- Record important design and technical decisions.
- Record failed approaches and why they failed.
- Record successful approaches and the conditions under which they worked.
- Update project documentation when implementation or decisions change.
- Preserve all explicit human instructions and constraints.
- Keep documentation consistent with the current verified project state.

Do not repeatedly rediscover settled decisions when reliable project notes already exist. Do not treat an unverified assumption as a recorded fact.

## 11.8 Human Interaction and Escalation

- Ask the human only when genuinely blocked or when requirements are materially ambiguous.
- Discover missing information independently before asking.
- Do not request unnecessary approval for reversible, routine, or implementation-level decisions.
- Do not request step-by-step authorization.
- Report major decisions that materially affect scope, architecture, requirements, or project direction.
- Report completion with evidence of verification.
- Clearly report unresolved problems, blockers, assumptions, and limitations.

When a choice is reversible and does not conflict with human instructions, select the most suitable option autonomously and document the decision. When requirements are genuinely ambiguous and different interpretations would materially change the result, ask before committing to the consequential choice.

## 11.9 Security and Preservation

- Protect credentials, tokens, private keys, and other secrets.
- Require explicit human approval before irreversible actions.
- Require explicit human approval before external publication or release.
- Restrict execution of untrusted code.
- Inspect unfamiliar scripts, plugins, assets, and dependencies before running or integrating them.
- Preserve original project files and source material.
- Prefer backups, version control, reversible edits, and isolated experiments.
- Never overwrite or destroy original work merely to simplify implementation.

Autonomy does not authorize credential disclosure, unsafe code execution, irreversible destruction, or external publication.

## 11.10 Completion and Delivery Contract

Every completed task must include, as applicable:

1. The finished artifact, implementation, or modified project files.
2. A concise execution summary describing what changed.
3. Test results and the verification method used.
4. Known limitations and unresolved problems.
5. Important decisions or assumptions made during execution.

Never claim that a feature, integration, test, or deliverable succeeded unless the result was actually verified.

If execution is blocked, deliver the completed portion where useful and identify precisely what remains blocked and why.

# 12. EXECUTION PRIORITIES FOR THIS PROJECT

Apply the autonomous execution framework to the development priorities in Section 10.

- Treat the existing world, civilization, settlement, POI, enemy, geography, exploration, and editing requirements as the project baseline.
- Inspect the selected development kits and their actual capabilities before deciding how to implement a feature.
- Research before choosing architecture or committing to a technical dependency.
- Maintain a record of kit capabilities, compatibility findings, limitations, and integration decisions.
- Break world-building and systems work into independently executable tasks where practical.
- Verify each phase before treating it as complete or making dependent phases rely on it.
- Preserve the original design requirements unless a change is justified, documented, and approved where necessary.
- Use experiments and comparative testing to resolve uncertain implementation choices.
- Maintain project notes and update this document as verified decisions are made.
- Deliver working, tested project changes whenever the environment and available tools permit; do not substitute plans for executable work.

## 12.1 Phase Exit Requirements

A phase may be marked complete only when:
- Its listed deliverables exist.
- Relevant integration requirements have been checked.
- Its objective success criteria have been tested.
- Known failures and limitations have been recorded.
- Documentation reflects the verified result.

A phase that has been started but not verified must remain marked as in progress.

## 12.2 Authority and Constraint Order

When making execution decisions, apply constraints in this order:

1. Explicit human instructions and approvals.
2. Security, preservation, and irreversible-action restrictions in this document.
3. The functional game-design requirements in Sections 1–10.
4. The autonomous execution and verification requirements in Sections 11–12.
5. The agent's implementation preferences.

An agent may choose methods autonomously, but may not silently change the intended game, remove requirements, or reinterpret a design decision as permission to expand scope.
